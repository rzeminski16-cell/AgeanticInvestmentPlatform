Second-quarter net income of $818m is the reference point for that judgement.

Two structural points frame durability. The company describes itself as a financial intermediary whose liquidity risk arises whenever the cash flows associated with assets and liabilities differ — the cash flow statement is an outcome of that mismatch management rather than an independent measure of it. And a substantial swap book, with cash flow hedges on variable-rate commercial real estate and commercial and industrial loans and fair value hedges on fixed-rate long-term borrowings, sits between market rates and realised interest cash flows, stabilising them within the hedged horizon. Full-year cash flow detail for fiscal 2026 is not yet available; the half-year pattern above is the operative evidence.

### Recorded Caveats

- Residual income equals the dividend-discount value only under clean surplus — that every gain and loss passes through profit, so book value moves only by earnings less dividends. A bank's available-for-sale securities move through other comprehensive income instead, so a book value carrying unrealised losses is treated here as fully earning.
- No value is placed on anything beyond the explicit forecast: the excess return is assumed competed away at the end of it.
- The two terminal treatments are opposite claims about competition, not two estimates of one number. Fading to nothing says a bank's excess return is competed away at the end of the forecast; perpetual growth says it never is. Both are shown because choosing between them is a judgement about banking, and presenting either alone would present that judgement as arithmetic.
- The valuation assumes the bank earns above its cost of equity in perpetuity. Competition has historically removed such a spread.
- This request carries no authored scenarios, so the valuation is the base case and the two grids beside it. Scenarios are written, not generated: nothing here invents a bear case the operator did not argue for.

### Commentary

The appraisal above is equity-side by construction, and that is the right shape for this company. A bank's operating cash flows are inseparable from its funding and lending decisions — core deposits are the most significant source of funding, and balance sheet growth consumes and releases cash for reasons that have nothing to do with value creation — so a firm-level free cash flow structure would carry no economic meaning here. No such structure was forced onto the bank: what is rendered is a premium to book value earned by the spread between return on equity and cost of equity, converted to a value per share on shares outstanding.

Everything in the result reduces to one judgement: whether M&T earns more on its book equity than shareholders require, and for how long. The two value-per-share figures are the same appraisal under two terminal treatments. In the competed-away case the excess return decays to nothing, so the premium to book value captures only the transitional period. In the perpetuity case the spread is held open indefinitely and the premium compounds. The distance between them is not a disagreement about near-term earnings power; it is the price of franchise durability. A reader who believes a deposit franchise of this kind is defensible should weight the perpetuity figure; one who expects competition to erode pricing should weight the other.

The conditioning inputs deserve to be read as declared assumptions rather than observations. Cost of equity is assembled from a risk-free rate, an equity risk premium and a beta; terminal growth and the payout ratio bind mainly on the perpetuity variant, where an assumed growth rate close to cost of equity magnifies the terminal contribution. The recorded return on equity was 9.8 percent for FY2025 against 8.9 percent for FY2024 — an improving trend, but at a level close to most defensible cost-of-equity assumptions. When the assumed sustainable return sits near the required return, the premium to book value is both modest and unstable: a small revision to either side moves the appraisal disproportionately, and the two value-per-share outputs should be read as a range, not as competing point estimates.

The base of that premium is book value, not tangible book. Common equity of $27.946 billion at June 30, 2026 carries goodwill of $8.465 billion and other intangible assets of $48 million; expressed on a tangible basis, the same value per share would represent a materially wider premium, and the return the bank must sustain to justify it is correspondingly higher. Reported equity also mixes common and preferred capital, with a preferred series redeemed in February 2026 and a further series issued in July 2026, so the bridge from equity value to common value per share is sensitive to how preferred is handled. Book value likewise absorbs accumulated other comprehensive income, including unrealised marks on available-for-sale securities and cash flow hedges, which makes the anchor itself move with rate conditions.

Share count is the other lever, and it is moving. Shares outstanding stood at 144,416,262 at July 31, 2026 against 146,445,060 at April 30, 2026, following repurchases of $1.698 billion in the first six months of 2026 under a program the board authorised in March 2026 that replaced the prior authorisation. Buybacks executed below the appraised value per share accrete to continuing holders and executed above it dilute them, so the repurchase pace is itself a claim by management about which of the two terminal cases is nearer the truth. Diluted earnings of $5.32 per share against a declared dividend of $1.50 per share in the second quarter of 2026 leaves the payout assumption in the appraisal well covered by current earnings, with retained capital available to fund either growth or further repurchase.

Two limits should be stated. This appraisal is internally consistent but unbenchmarked: it carries no cross-check of price to tangible book against return on tangible common equity for comparable banks, so it establishes what the assumptions imply rather than whether the market shares them. And if the sustainable return converges toward the required return — through funding cost, credit normalisation or regulatory capital demands — the value per share migrates toward the competed-away figure, which is the conservative reading and, in our judgement, the more prudent anchor absent evidence of a widening return advantage.
