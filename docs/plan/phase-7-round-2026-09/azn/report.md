## Business Overview

### Commentary

AstraZeneca is a biopharmaceutical group whose business rests on developing, manufacturing and commercialising prescription medicines sold to healthcare systems, physicians and patients across established and emerging markets. The commercial risk disclosures reviewed set out how the company is paid and where that payment can be threatened: government and health-authority pricing controls, national reimbursement regimes and initiatives such as the EU Joint Clinical Assessment and the US Inflation Reduction Act all bear directly on price and volume, while newer health-equalisation policies are pushing some countries to raise drug spending in support of R&D costs. Commercial success is described as depending on manufacturing enough of a given product, securing patient access and diagnosis, defending intellectual property, and maintaining a differentiated product profile against changing prescribing habits. The group flags that emerging markets carry added difficulty from economic and political volatility, weaker protection against counterfeiting, corruption and fraud, and the risk of inadvertent breaches of local or international law. On the regulatory side, delays in approvals can push out the timing of when a product reaches the market and therefore when revenue is recognised, and post-approval obligations such as additional clinical trials or safety testing can raise costs or, if new safety data emerge, lead to labelling changes that narrow a product's approved use. Separately, where the group disposes of assets or businesses without retaining a significant ongoing economic interest, the resulting income is booked within other operating income and expense rather than within core revenue.

The evidence available for this note does not include a segment or therapy-area breakdown of revenue, nor a description of the group's geographic revenue mix or manufacturing and commercial footprint, so no split by product line or region is presented here. What is available is a single set of full-year 2025 income-statement figures. These figures indicate a business generating substantial gross margin relative to revenue, with research and development representing a large recurring call on that margin alongside selling and administrative costs, consistent with a company whose commercial fortunes hinge on continued product approvals and the pricing and access dynamics described above. Without further detail on the underlying revenue composition, it is not possible from this evidence to attribute the scale of revenue to specific products, therapy areas or geographies, and that gap should be read as a limit on how far this overview can characterise where AstraZeneca's revenue actually originates.

## Industry & Competitive Positioning

### Commentary

The global innovative-pharmaceutical industry in which AstraZeneca competes is a concentrated, R&D-intensive oligopoly of large-cap diversified players—Pfizer, Novartis, Merck & Co., Bristol Myers Squibb, GSK, Sanofi, Eli Lilly and Johnson & Johnson among them—each defending broad primary-care and specialty portfolios against patent expiry through pipeline replacement and business development. Pricing power across the group is increasingly set by policy rather than negotiation: in the US, the Inflation Reduction Act's structure creates prospective downward pressure on branded-drug pricing and Part D economics, while in the EU the Joint Clinical Assessment regime and national price-control mechanisms add further constraint, and AstraZeneca's own disclosure references 'health equalisation initiatives' pushing some countries toward higher drug-spending contributions. The evidence available here does not specify which AstraZeneca molecules sit on any Medicare selected-drug list, what maximum fair prices or effective dates apply, whether a most-favoured-nation or tariff action is currently in force, or the outcome of any China National Healthcare Security Administration volume-based procurement round or National Reimbursement Drug List adjustment touching named AstraZeneca products—so no product-specific timing or price-outcome can be asserted, only that these mechanisms are industry-wide constraints AstraZeneca is exposed to as a matter of policy design.

### Competitive Position

AstraZeneca operates at a scale consistent with the large-cap comparator set: full-year 2025 revenue of approximately $58,739 million supported gross profit of about $48,106 million, operating income of roughly $13,743 million and net income near $10,225 million, underpinning continued investment in oncology, biopharmaceuticals and rare disease franchises. That scale gives it room to absorb geography-specific pricing shocks—Part D redesign, EU JCA outcomes, or China procurement rounds—better than smaller specialty peers, but the company's own filings flag ongoing inquiries into drug marketing and pricing practices and identify price-control measures, patient access and prescribing-habit shifts among the principal threats to commercial execution of new launches. Regulatory delay risk compounds this: post-approval study requirements or labelling changes following new safety or efficacy data can alter a product's competitive standing after approval. Absent product-level evidence, AstraZeneca's positioning should be read as broadly resilient on scale and diversification, with the direction of pricing pressure clearly downward across major markets but its precise incidence on individual assets not established here.

### Industry Trends

- US Inflation Reduction Act pricing and Part D redesign mechanics continue to weigh on branded-drug economics industry-wide.
- EU Joint Clinical Assessment and national price-control mechanisms add further downward pricing pressure in Europe.
- Some governments are pursuing 'health equalisation' initiatives intended to raise drug-spending contributions toward R&D costs.
- China's volume-based procurement and national reimbursement list processes remain a structural pricing risk, though no AstraZeneca-specific round outcome is evidenced here.
- Ongoing regulatory and law-enforcement inquiries into drug marketing and pricing practices are a recurring feature across major pharmaceutical companies, including AstraZeneca.

## Earnings Quality

### Commentary

Reported profit for the year to 31 December 2025 looks cash-backed. The gap is not explained by an outsized non-cash add-back: share-based compensation of $719 million is small beside both the operating cash figure and revenue of $58,739 million. Working capital was not a swing factor either; the recorded movement of -$386.6 million is immaterial against that revenue base, so the year's cash conversion does not rest on receivable or inventory timing.

Reinvestment and distributions both sit comfortably inside operating cash. Capital expenditure was $2,810 million, leaving ample coverage of the $4,971 million paid in dividends without recourse to borrowing: proceeds from debt of $15 million were negligible against repayments of $2,029 million, and share repurchases are recorded at nil. Shareholder returns were funded from cash the business produced rather than from balance sheet capacity, which is supportive of earnings durability.

The tax line offers less comfort — not because of its level, a charge of $2,169 million against pre-tax income of $12,402 million, but because cash taxes actually paid are not separately available in the material at hand, so the charge cannot be tested against outflows.

The principal quality caveats are judgement-based rather than cash-based. Management identifies revenue recognition, the expensing of internal development costs, impairment reviews and useful economic lives of intangible assets, business combinations and goodwill, litigation liabilities, operating segments, employee benefits and taxation as its key judgements and significant estimates, and notes that actual results could differ from those estimates. For a group whose asset base is dominated by acquired and internally generated intangibles, impairment testing and useful-life assumptions are the levers with the most power over reported profit, and neither shows up in cash flow. Legal exposure is similar: the group states that the probability and amount of any loss are often difficult to ascertain, that determining when an adverse outcome becomes probable is a key judgement, and that the more significant matters involve highly complex issues. Timing choices there move operating profit without moving cash in the same period.

Only one annual period is available here, so the accrual-to-cash relationship cannot be traced across years; the fiscal 2025 reading is a level, not a trend. On that level, the cash evidence is favourable and the residual risk to earnings quality sits in non-cash estimates — intangible carrying values and litigation provisioning — rather than in the conversion of profit into cash.

### Commentary

The discount rate is the first thing to test, because both enterprise values scale off it and neither is robust to a small change in it. The cost of equity applied is 6.31%. The pre-tax cost of debt is 5.65% and the after-tax cost of debt 4.52%. Equity is therefore compensated barely above lenders in a business whose own reporting names sales erosion curves following patent expiry, probability of technical and regulatory success, and market volume, share and pricing among the key assumptions in its discounted cash flow work. That ordering is not defensible. The driver is a beta of 0.28: at that level the equity risk premium contributes little, and no plausible risk-free rate repairs the gap. Debt weight at market value is 10.5%, so the WACC sits close to the cost of equity and inherits the problem almost one for one. The company's disclosed post-tax weighted average cost of capital, and the risk-adjusted post-tax range used for value-in-use impairment testing, both stand above the cost of equity used here — evidence from its own hand that this rate is low. The value per share figures above should be read as the top of a range, not the middle of one.

The second issue is the cash flow being discounted. In FY2025 the company generated operating cash flow of $14.6bn and spent $2.81bn on capital expenditure against revenue of $58.7bn, producing operating income of $13.7bn. The forecast carries revenue materially above that base, yet the EBIT margin input leaves forecast operating profit only modestly ahead of the FY2025 outcome — margin compression — while capex intensity sits below depreciation intensity across the horizon, which describes a business investing less than it consumes. The two assumptions pull opposite ways: a company whose margin is eroding is not usually one that can also stop reinvesting. The free cash flow being discounted therefore runs ahead of what the FY2025 cash statement supports, and capex intensity is the most load-bearing input in the block. Moving it towards the FY2025 relationship between spending and the asset base would reduce both enterprise values, and would do so before any change to the discount rate.

Third, the two terminal conventions do not agree, and the gap is not a rounding matter: enterprise value is $295.4bn on the exit multiple and $500.2bn on Gordon growth, with the terminal value share high under both. So the answer is largely a choice of convention, and it should be resolved rather than presented as a coin toss. We anchor on the exit-multiple case, whose equity value is $271.5bn, and treat the Gordon figure as an upper bound with no independent standing. The reason is mechanical. Gordon growth divides by the spread between WACC and terminal growth; at a WACC set on the cost of equity described above, that spread is narrow and the resulting value is hypersensitive to precisely the input we have just argued is understated. The exit multiple prices the terminal year off a multiple instead, and the perpetual growth it implies can be read against terminal growth as a sanity check on whether the multiple is doing more work than the fundamentals allow. Anchoring there does not manufacture an investment case, and it is not meant to: on these inputs the discounted cash flow does not establish a case on its own, and the shares are not shown to be materially mispriced by it.

On what is priced in, we assign no pipeline-attributed share of implied growth. Any split between disclosed in-market products running down their erosion schedules and the unapproved portfolio would be an assumption dressed as a measurement, and we prefer to leave it unstated than to publish a residual that carries the error of every input above it. What would change the conclusion is narrow and specific: a cost of equity set at or above the company's own disclosed discount rates, and a capex intensity consistent with the FY2025 spending pattern rather than below it. Until both hold, the fair values rendered above are ceilings, the exit-multiple case is the one to reason from, and the Gordon case should carry no weight in a decision.
