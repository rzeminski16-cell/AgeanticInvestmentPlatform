## Investment Thesis

The audited FY2025 base is clear enough: revenue of USD 58.7 billion, gross profit of USD 48.1 billion, and operating income of USD 13.7 billion struck after research and development of USD 14.2 billion and selling, general and administrative costs of USD 19.9 billion. The valuation on record, an equity value of USD 247.7 billion, is produced by compounding at 9.05% and discounting at a 6.29% cost of equity, and neither input is anchored to an external estimate in the material before us. We therefore put the thesis as three falsifiable propositions, and mark plainly where the record cannot yet test them.

## Industry & Competitive Positioning

### Commentary

AstraZeneca competes in the global branded pharmaceutical industry, a structure dominated by a small number of research-intensive multinationals that compete on patent-protected biologics and small-molecule portfolios across oncology, respiratory, cardiovascular, and immunology. The company's own risk disclosures identify continuing pricing pressure from policy change as a structural feature of the industry, naming the EU Joint Clinical Assessment regime, the US Inflation Reduction Act, and emerging "health equalisation" initiatives that press governments to raise drug spending as factors reshaping the global pricing landscape. Beyond this general characterisation, the evidence available here does not include the specific rule-based mechanics that would normally frame US exposure: it does not show the CMS selected-drug list by initial price applicability year, the published maximum fair prices for any AstraZeneca product, the Part D redesign manufacturer discount schedule, or the Federal Register status (proposed, final, or enjoined) of most-favoured-nation pricing actions or Section 232 pharmaceutical tariff proceedings. Nor does it contain any AstraZeneca press release or 6-K filing disclosing a bilateral pricing undertaking, a direct-to-patient pricing programme, or a tariff or onshoring commitment. Consequently, no view is offered on those channels; what can be said is that price control measures and prescribing-habit shifts imposed by governments and healthcare authorities are cited by the company itself as a risk to commercial execution, alongside patient access, diagnosis rates, and erosion of intellectual-property protection.

### Competitive Position

Within this structure, AstraZeneca sits alongside a small set of comparably scaled, R&D-driven branded pharmaceutical companies: Pfizer and Merck, with broad oncology and vaccines portfolios overlapping AstraZeneca's; Bristol Myers Squibb, sharing oncology and immunology focus and similar patent-cliff dynamics; Novartis and Sanofi, European large-caps with overlapping respiratory, cardiovascular, and biologics franchises; GSK, a UK peer weighted more toward vaccines and consumer health; Novo Nordisk, concentrated in diabetes and cardiometabolic care where AstraZeneca also competes; and Eli Lilly, overlapping in oncology and cardiometabolic franchises but more US-centric. AstraZeneca's position within this group rests on portfolio breadth across multiple therapeutic areas rather than concentration in one, which the company itself frames as a source of resilience against single-market or single-product pricing shocks, alongside the general policy pressures noted above. The company also discloses legal provisions tied in part to pricing, sales and marketing practices, and competition-law matters connected to its commercial disputes, an ongoing cost of operating across many pricing and regulatory regimes simultaneously. No figures are available here to quantify AstraZeneca's US revenue exposure to selected-drug pricing, Part D discount mechanics, or tariff proceedings, so this section can characterise the general contours of the industry's pricing environment but cannot size AstraZeneca's specific exposure to any single rule-based mechanism at this time.

### Commentary

The reported record available for this analysis is a single completed fiscal year — the twelve months to 31 December 2025 — together with the balance sheet at that date. Prior-year comparatives and the half-year interim accounts are not in evidence, so no multi-year trend in revenue, margin or research intensity can be asserted here, and the product-line detail that would show how earlier exclusivity losses actually landed is likewise absent. What the single year does support is a clean baseline for the margin question.

Revenue of USD 58.7bn was struck against cost of revenue of USD 10.6bn, leaving gross profit of USD 48.1bn. The overwhelming share of the top line therefore survives the cost of goods line, as it should for a branded, patent-protected mix; the interesting part of the margin question for this company sits below the gross line, not at it.

Two operating cost blocks dominate that space. Research and development expense was USD 14.2bn and selling, general and administrative expense USD 19.9bn, and after those charges reported operating income was USD 13.7bn. The structure is the key to the exclusivity question: gross margin on a branded portfolio is high and slow-moving, while research and commercial spend are committed ahead of the revenue they support. In our view a loss of exclusivity in an established product line removes gross profit immediately while the two cost blocks do not contract in step, so reported operating margin absorbs the shock first and recovers only as spend is redirected. That asymmetry, rather than mix within the gross margin, is where operating margin is decided.

Below operating income the financing position is a modest net cost: interest expense of USD 1.7bn against interest income of USD 360m. Pre-tax income was USD 12.4bn, a tax charge of USD 2.2bn left net income of USD 10.2bn, and diluted earnings per share were USD 6.54.

The balance sheet is intangible-heavy, as an acquisitive research company's tends to be. Of total assets of USD 114.1bn at the year-end date, intangible assets were USD 37.8bn and goodwill USD 21.2bn. The company itself notes that its statement of financial position contains significant investments in intangible assets, including goodwill, and that its ability to realise value on them depends on regulatory approvals, market acceptance, competition and legal developments — the same dependency chain that governs the margin path. Liquidity is worked hard: cash and equivalents stood at USD 5.7bn, current assets at USD 28.7bn and current liabilities at USD 30.6bn.

Two reporting-basis points frame any comparison over time. First, results are reported in US dollars while the key currencies of product sales and costs include the euro, Chinese renminbi, pound sterling, Japanese yen and Swedish krona, so translation alone moves reported growth and reported margin. In results published in April 2026 the company indicated that, were exchange rates to hold at the average seen in March 2026, reported total revenue for the current financial year would carry a low single-digit percentage positive impact relative to performance at constant exchange rates, with core earnings per share growth broadly similar to the constant-currency figure. Reported and constant-currency margin bridges should therefore not be read interchangeably. Second, the comparative we would have liked — a peer's reported product-line erosion through a loss-of-exclusivity cycle, on a labelled accounting basis — is not present in the evidence available, so the erosion profile below is a structural argument from the cost base rather than a calibrated precedent.

On this single-year baseline, the judgement we would carry forward is that the gross line is not the variable to watch. The distance between gross profit of USD 48.1bn and operating income of USD 13.7bn is the whole of the margin debate, and it is occupied by two spend lines that management sets rather than by cost of goods.

## Earnings Quality

### Commentary

Read every margin in this report on a Reported IFRS basis unless it is labelled Core. For the financial year ended December 2025, AstraZeneca reported revenue of $58,739m, operating income of $13,743m and net income of $10,225m, with basic earnings per share of $6.60 against diluted earnings per share of $6.54. The Core bridge itemising acquired-intangible amortisation, restructuring, legal charges and contingent-consideration fair-value movements is not before us, so the basis label matters: the two presentations answer the margin question differently and an unlabelled margin is not interpretable.

Cash backing for the reported number is good. Operating cash flow of $14,575m ran above reported net income of $10,225m, so the year's accrual profit was more than covered by cash generated in the same period. The recorded working-capital movement was a drag of negative $386.6m, small beside operating cash flow and not the pattern one would expect from a receivables build of the kind that US gross-to-net rebate accrual timing can obscure. Capital expenditure of $2,810m and dividends paid of $4,971m were both met out of operating cash flow; debt proceeds of $15m against repayments of $2,029m confirm that financing was a use of cash rather than a prop under it. Share-based compensation of $719m was added back as a non-cash charge, and with the repurchase line recorded at nil for the period that dilution is not bought back, which is why diluted earnings per share sits below basic.

On the character of the adjusting items: the annual filing describes the Group's legal proceedings as typical to its business, carries legal provisions at end-December 2025, and flags the timing of recognition once an adverse outcome becomes probable as a key judgement, so legal charges are recurring in substance rather than exceptional. Acquired-intangible amortisation is structurally recurring too. The Fusion transaction brought in products-in-development intangibles fair valued on probability-of-technical-and-regulatory-success and peak-year-sales assumptions, one programme written off in full in December 2024 when the related activities were terminated, alongside regulatory milestone-based contingent consideration whose fair value moves through earnings. In our judgement charges of this type are a running cost of an acquisition-led research model rather than genuine one-offs, which makes Core the flattering presentation and Reported the conservative one. Earnings quality on the cash test is sound; the open question sits in the gap between the two presentations, not in the cash.

### Red Flags

- Adjusting items excluded from Core — acquired-intangible amortisation, development-asset impairment and contingent-consideration fair-value movements — are recurring in character, so Core overstates underlying economics relative to Reported IFRS.
- Legal provisioning rests on a stated key judgement about when an adverse outcome becomes probable, which can move charges between periods.
- Share-based compensation is added back as a non-cash charge while the repurchase line stands at nil for the period, leaving the dilution it creates unoffset.
- Margins are not comparable across bases; any margin quoted without a Core or Reported IFRS label should be treated as unusable.

### Recorded Caveats

- Book equity was used as the equity weight because no market capitalisation was available. Book value is what the shares were issued and retained for rather than what they are worth, and for a profitable company it is usually far lower — so the equity weight is understated, the debt weight overstated, and the resulting WACC too low. Every valuation discounted at it is correspondingly too high.
- More than three quarters of the enterprise value is terminal value. The explicit forecast is contributing little, so the answer rests almost entirely on the terminal assumption rather than on the projected years a reader can check.
- The two terminal methods disagree by more than a quarter. That is information, not an error: they are two different guesses about the same unknowable quantity, and the distance between them is the honest width of the answer.
- The bridge from enterprise value to equity value is net debt alone. Associate holdings, minority interests and pension deficits are not read off the filings by this build, so a business carrying material non-operating items is valued as though it does not.

### Commentary

The recorded outputs are an enterprise value of about USD 271.6bn and an equity value of about USD 247.7bn, with implied upside of negative 4.5 percent. Read literally that says the shares are already fully valued. The more useful question is what the number rests on, because on inspection the build is generous in several places at once and still does not clear the price.

Start with the base. The first forecast year carries free cash flow of about USD 20.5bn. The FY2025 filed cash flow statement records operating cash flow of USD 14.6bn and capital expenditure of USD 2.8bn, so the forecast year-one figure exceeds the realised operating inflow before any investment is deducted at all. The composition of that cash flow is the second issue. Forecast EBITDA of about USD 26.5bn stands above the forecast free cash flow, but Revenue growth and EBIT margin do not by themselves account for the cash. The Depreciation intensity assumption sits well above the Capex intensity assumption, and Working capital intensity contributes a small release, with the change in working capital recorded at negative USD 387m. It also sits awkwardly against management's own statement that FY 2026 expenditure on property, plant and equipment and software-related intangible assets will rise by roughly a third on FY 2025, driven by manufacturing expansion and investment in systems and technology — a capital path that argues for higher, not lower, investment intensity through the forecast.

Third, the discount rate. Beta of 0.27 drives a cost of equity of 6.29 percent, blended with an after-tax cost of debt of 4.5 percent under the recorded Equity weight and Debt weight to give the WACC applied throughout. For a business whose value turns on trial readouts and exclusivity, that is a light charge for risk. The 20-F discloses a company post-tax weighted average cost of capital above that level, and applies higher risk-adjusted post-tax rates again when testing intangible assets for impairment. Anchored nearer the company's own cost of capital, the equity value falls appreciably. We therefore read the recorded equity value as the top of a defensible band rather than a central estimate.

Fourth, growth. The Revenue growth assumption compounds at 9.05 percent. What supports it here is qualitative: quarterly commentary on patient demand, geographic expansion, strong uptake following an adult indication launch, and conversion from Soliris across gMG, NMOSD, aHUS and PNH. There is no product-level revenue split or exclusivity schedule in the record to size either the compounding or the erosion that offsets it, while the company's own impairment work names sales erosion curves following patent expiry, probability of technical and regulatory success and peak year sales among its key sensitivities. High-single-digit compounding across the forecast horizon is an assumption, not a projection the filings underwrite.

The terminal block decides the rest. Gordon growth terminal value of about USD 510.4bn against exit-multiple terminal value of about USD 318.2bn is a spread wide enough that Terminal value share — Gordon growth and Terminal value share — Exit multiple describe two different valuations, and the Value per share outputs inherit that split. Against this, the recorded implied terminal growth of about 0.16 percent and implied exit multiple of 19.2 times describe the assumption set consistent with the current ADR price. That is the 'already in the price' test, and its answer is uncomfortable for the build rather than for the shares: near-zero perpetual growth is what the price requires, yet with an inflated starting cash flow, a light discount rate and 9.05 percent compounding the model still lands below the price. Correcting the base or the rate widens that shortfall. We would not use this output as the primary valuation anchor.
