## Executive Summary

### Summary

AstraZeneca enters the period with FY2025 revenue of $58.74bn and operating income of $13.74bn after an R&D load of $14.23bn. The evidence establishes that structure but not the three questions that most determine value: exclusivity exposure inside the horizon, what the price implies for oncology, and the margin effect of US pricing policy — income-statement coverage ends at the December 2025 year end, with no product, segment or policy detail. Open is whether the recorded ten-year revenue CAGR of 9.05%, measured on USD reported revenue, survives once exclusivity loss and pipeline contribution are counted separately rather than assumed.

### Key Points

- Exclusivity exposure cannot be quantified here: no product-level sales or expiry dates are in evidence, and the latest stored period detail is Q2 2025 revenue of $14.46bn with operating income of $3.51bn.
- The ten-year CAGR of 9.05% was delivered by assets that were pipeline during that decade and by brands now nearing expiry. Extrapolating it while crediting nothing to unapproved pipeline and deducting nothing for exclusivity is not conservative; it counts the same history twice.
- The discount rate is load-bearing, and the recorded cost of equity of 6.46% sits barely above the pre-tax cost of debt of 5.65% — 4.52% after tax — because measured beta is 0.277. Discounted values on that basis are conditional on an unnormalised rate, not base cases; average debt is $29.96bn.
- No implied-expectations test on oncology can be run: the evidence carries neither segment revenue nor a consensus range to bound dispersion. The qualitative signal is management's reported positive pivotal readouts, including tozorakimab in COPD and efzimfotase alfa in hypophosphatasia.
- No US pricing-policy margin effect is modelled, since no incremental-margin assumption can be sourced. The structure — FY2025 gross profit of $48.11bn against SG&A of $19.93bn — indicates that price-led revenue loss would fall largely on operating income.

## The Case For and the Case Against

### What the Two Cases Turn On

Both cases begin from the same FY2025 record and divide on three points: whether ten-year compound growth of 9.05% describes a franchise that renews itself or a cohort of brands nearing exclusivity expiry; whether the operating margin survives pricing pressure; and whether a cost of equity built on a measured beta of 0.277 is the right rate at which to capitalise either answer.

### The Case For

**A record base year, earned on volume rather than price:** FY2025 revenue of $58.7bn converted into gross profit of $48.1bn and operating income of $13.7bn, after $14.2bn of research spending charged in full against the year. The full-year results attribute the growth to continued patient demand and geographic expansion, with sustained double-digit volume growth and expanded class leadership in the lead franchises — a mix that does not depend on list price holding. Carry that margin across the forecast and the valuation is struck on the best operating margin in the record rather than on an assumption about a mix shift.[^28]

**Compounding has been the rule for a decade:** The compound revenue growth rate over the ten years to FY2025 is 9.05%, achieved across successive launch cycles rather than in one step. The Q1 2026 statement attributes rising selling and administrative expense to ongoing and future launches and to supporting growth in existing brands — spending pointed at new volume, not at defending old price. An advocate would argue the forecast should carry the upper end of the delivered range, not its average.[^29]

**Late-stage readouts the forecast does not pay for:** The $14.2bn of research expense in FY2025 is charged as cost while its output sits outside the revenue base. Phase III results announced to the market during 2026 carry no stored revenue attached to them anywhere in this analysis, so whatever they are worth is upside the forecast does not claim. Equally, nothing here establishes what the share price already discounts for them, and we do not assert a direction on that point.[^30]

### The Case Against

**The growth record cannot be spent twice:** The 9.05% ten-year rate was delivered by medicines that were themselves pipeline assets during that window and by brands now approaching exclusivity expiry. Extrapolating it forward while attributing nothing to unapproved assets is not conservatism; it counts the same engine twice. The evidence in hand neither quantifies exclusivity losses falling inside the forecast horizon nor separates them from competitive displacement by rival launches, so the drag is unmeasured rather than absent — and a forecast built on the slowest year in the record is the honest bound.[^37]

**The discount rate carries almost the whole answer:** A cost of equity of 6.46% sits barely above a pre-tax cost of debt of 5.65%, implying that equity holders in a patent-exposed pharmaceutical demand nearly nothing over its bondholders; the driver is a measured beta of 0.277, defensible as measurement and implausible as a required return. With the fifth forecast year discounted at 0.738, terminal value dominates the result, so every enterprise value and per-share figure in this report should be read as conditional on that unnormalised rate rather than as a settled base case. Normalising the rate upward would cut the value materially.[^37]

**Margin is measured on a basis that has moved:** Core measures including EBITDA, net debt, gross margin, operating margin, tax rate and constant-currency growth are non-GAAP and cannot be derived directly from the consolidated statements, and the gross margin definition itself was changed effective 1 January 2025. Quarterly growth reflects order timing in tender markets, and Q4 2025 carried an unfavourable gross-to-net adjustment. Against operating income of $13.7bn, selling and administrative expense of $19.9bn leaves little cushion if launch spending continues to run ahead of launch revenue.[^28]

**The price-cut arithmetic is open:** Where revenue is surrendered to negotiated or mandated price cuts, the effect on operating income depends on the incremental margin of the units given up, and no figure available here supplies it; the direction of revenue is down, but the direction of margin is not determined by anything on file and we do not assert one. Interest expense of $1.7bn set against pre-tax income of $12.4bn means the earnings line carries some leverage into whatever that answer turns out to be.[^37]

## Industry & Competitive Positioning

### Commentary

AstraZeneca competes in an industry structure dominated by a handful of large, diversified originators that combine broad primary-care, oncology, respiratory, cardiovascular and vaccine/immunology franchises with substantial biologics and biosimilar exposure. That structure exposes every incumbent, AstraZeneca included, to two distinct erosion channels that should be tracked separately rather than conflated. The first is US and other-market pricing mechanics — statutory price-setting, rebate obligations, tariff and most-favoured-nation policy instruments, and volume-based procurement in China — which compress realised price on covered products independent of any competitor's clinical progress. The second is competitive displacement — rival branded assets reaching approval, filing or read-out in the same indications, plus generic and biosimilar entry — which erodes share through substitution rather than price mechanics. The evidence available for this note does not extend to the specific Medicare selected-drug and maximum fair price effective-date lists, Part D redesign manufacturer-liability schedules, Federal Register most-favoured-nation or tariff dockets, or 340B/Medicaid rebate detail by product, nor to FDA approval records or clinicaltrials.gov read-outs identifying named rival assets against AstraZeneca's in-line oncology products or Phase III pipeline; that gap should be read as a limit on what can be verified here, not as an absence of exposure. What the record does support is that AstraZeneca, like peer originators, has disclosed generic competition in China tied to anticipated volume-based procurement implementation, and, as is common among major prescription pharmaceutical companies, is subject to multiple ongoing government inquiries into drug marketing and pricing practices.

### Competitive Position

Within this landscape, AstraZeneca sits among the largest diversified originators, comparable in scale and patent-cliff dynamics to Pfizer, Merck, Novartis, GSK, Sanofi, Eli Lilly and Bristol Myers Squibb, each carrying overlapping oncology, cardiovascular, respiratory, immunology or vaccine exposure and similar global regulatory footprints. AstraZeneca's own scale is evidenced by FY2025 revenue of approximately $58,739 million, gross profit of approximately $48,106 million and operating income of approximately $13,743 million. A structural feature distinguishing its go-to-market model in at least one product line is an alliance arrangement with Sanofi, under which revenue combines AstraZeneca's manufactured product sales to Sanofi with its share of gross profits and royalties on sales in major markets outside the US — a revenue-sharing structure that partially insulates ex-US economics from direct pricing mechanics but also means headline growth reflects order timing rather than underlying demand alone. Beyond this, the evidence does not support specific claims about where AstraZeneca's in-line oncology products or Phase III assets stand against named rival branded or biosimilar entrants, so its competitive position should be read as broadly comparable in exposure to the peer group above rather than differentiated on the record available here.

## Earnings Quality

### Commentary

Cash generation led reported profit in FY2025. Operating cash flow of $14,575m exceeded net income of $10,225m, so the year's accrual earnings were more than covered by cash from operations — the first test of earnings quality, and one AstraZeneca passes on the annual figures available. Capital expenditure of $2,810m absorbed only a minority of that inflow, leaving operating cash well ahead of plant and equipment needs; total investing outflow of $6,808m points to spending beyond fixed assets, intangible and milestone capitalisation among it.

Two items explain part of the gap between profit and cash. Share-based compensation of $719m is a non-cash charge within earnings, modest against net income and not a material distortion of the comparison. The recorded change in working capital is -$386.6m, small next to revenue of $58,739m — receivables and inventory do not appear, on annual aggregates, to be flattering the top line.

Financing behaviour supports the picture. The financing outflow of $7,544m comprised dividends of $4,971m and debt repayments of $2,029m against new debt proceeds of only $15m, with share repurchases recorded at nil. Distributions were funded from operating cash rather than borrowing, and the group closed the year with cash and equivalents of $5,711m.

Accounting judgement is where caution belongs. The full-year results describe regulatory and sales-related milestone payments capitalised rather than expensed, with further contingent payments possible, and sales-related amounts accrued and capitalised when considered probable by reference to the latest Group sales forecasts for approved indications. That treatment is permitted, but it ties carrying values and the timing of expense recognition to management's own forecasts: a more optimistic forecast capitalises more and depresses current-period cost. The cash leaves the business either way, which is why the operating-cash comparison above carries more weight than the margin line alone. The same filing notes a presentation change — product sales and alliance revenue remain separately presented but are aggregated into a new subtotal reflecting the growing importance of alliance revenue — which aids aggregation while complicating comparison of headline revenue across presentations.

Operating income of $13,743m converted to pre-tax income of $12,402m after an interest expense of $1,694m, and to diluted earnings per share of $6.54 after a tax charge of $2,169m; the spread between operating income and operating cash flow is in the expected direction for a business with large non-cash charges. The figures available cover a single fiscal year, so no trend in accrual ratios or cash conversion can be tested here, and the quality read is a point estimate rather than a track record. On that basis FY2025 earnings look cash-backed, with the principal open question being capitalisation policy on milestone payments rather than revenue recognition.

- Regulatory and sales-related milestone payments are capitalised rather than expensed, with sales-related amounts capitalised when management judges them probable against the latest Group sales forecasts — expense timing therefore depends on forecast judgement.

- A new revenue subtotal aggregating product sales and alliance revenue changes presentation and reduces comparability of headline revenue across periods.
- Only a single fiscal year of figures is available, so accrual ratios and cash-conversion trends cannot be tested against prior periods.

### Commentary

The two terminal treatments do not agree, and that disagreement is the main finding here rather than a rounding detail. On the exit multiple, enterprise value comes out at roughly $264.3bn and equity value at roughly $240.4bn; on Gordon growth, enterprise value is roughly $481.8bn. The distance between the two methods is wide enough that the value per share on each route should be read as two answers to two different questions about the terminal year, not as a band around a central case. Both routes place the bulk of value in the terminal value share, so the honest characterisation is that this is a judgement on terminal growth and on the perpetual growth the exit multiple implies, only loosely disciplined by the explicit forecast horizon.

The discount rate is the second lever and it is soft. Cost of equity sits at 6.46%, resting on a beta of 0.277 alongside the risk-free rate and equity risk premium in the block above. A beta that low holds the cost of equity close to the risk-free rate, and the terminal value is more sensitive to that single input than to anything in the operating forecast. Cost of debt is 5.65% pre-tax and 4.52% after tax, on a debt weight at market value of 10.4%, so the equity weight dominates and WACC sits near the cost of equity. The year-five discount factor of 0.738 shows how little the explicit horizon is compressed: distant forecast cash flows keep most of their face value, which is precisely why the terminal assumptions carry the outcome.

Underneath sit FY 2025 revenue of $58,739m, operating income of $13,743m, operating cash flow of $14,575m and capital expenditure of $2,810m. The working capital movement of -$386.6m is small against operating cash flow, so working capital intensity is a second-order input. Capex intensity is the input most likely to be too low: the company's own capital expenditure measure is broader than the cash-flow line and is guided materially higher for FY 2026 on manufacturing expansion and technology investment. Should that step-up prove durable rather than a single build cycle, the free cash flow path in the early forecast years is flattered.

On the revenue bridge, the ten-year CAGR of 9.0% anchors the revenue growth input but has no memory of dated exclusivity step-downs or of the separate competitive-displacement channel, both of which bite within the horizon. Labelled assumption — the revenue-to-margin mapping: nothing in the evidence establishes product-level cost of goods or therapy-area margin, so every unit of revenue lost to an exclusivity step-down or to displacement is assumed to release contribution at a single stated incremental margin applied uniformly across the bridge. That is an assumption, not a derived figure, and the EBIT margin path inherits it wholesale. The practical consequence cuts both ways: a uniform mapping understates margin damage where losses fall on mature, high-margin brands, and overstates it where the revenue is structurally lower-margin — the Beyfortus line, for instance, combines product sales to Sanofi with alliance revenue from a share of gross profits and royalties, an economically different mix. A reader who disagrees with the incremental margin should re-read the EBIT margin path before re-reading the growth rate.

Tax rate and depreciation intensity enter as stated above. Interest expense of $1,694m against average debt of roughly $30.0bn is broadly consistent with the modelled cost of debt, which is modest comfort on that component; shares outstanding is the only other bridge between equity value and value per share, so per-share figures move one-for-one with the terminal choice.

The view is taken in US dollars throughout, on dollar-reported figures, before any GBP translation; both value-per-share outputs are dollar amounts, and a sterling-based reader must apply translation separately. On balance we would weight the exit-multiple route over Gordon growth, because at a discount rate this close to terminal growth the perpetuity arithmetic becomes unstable, and treat the gap between the two as the live risk rather than as precision.
